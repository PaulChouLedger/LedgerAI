CHIEF COMPLAINT TRIGGERS:
This diagnosis should be considered when patient presents with:
  • pelvic pain
  • lower abdominal pain
  • vaginal discharge
  • painful intercourse
  • fever with pelvic pain

CLASSIC PRESENTATION:
ONSET: SUBACUTE onset over days to weeks. More common in sexually active women <25 years. LOCATION: Bilateral lower abdominal pain, may be unilateral. Deep pelvic pain. DURATION: CONSTANT pain lasting days to weeks. CHARACTER: DULL, ACHING, CONSTANT deep pelvic pain. May be SHARP with movement. AGGRAVATING: Intercourse (deep dyspareunia), jarring movements, pelvic exam causes severe tenderness (cervical motion tenderness). RELIEVING: Rest may help slightly. Antibiotics resolve pain over days. TIMING: CONSTANT baseline pain, worsens with movement or intercourse. SEVERITY: 5-8/10, moderate to severe. ASSOCIATED SYMPTOMS: Abnormal vaginal discharge (mucopurulent), fever (low-grade to high), dyspareunia, abnormal vaginal bleeding, dysuria. Cervical motion tenderness and adnexal tenderness on exam. May have right upper quadrant (RUQ) pain if Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome (perihepatic inflammation). KEY POSITIVES: Bilateral lower abdominal tenderness, cervical motion tenderness, abnormal discharge, sexual activity, young age. KEY NEGATIVES: Unilateral pain more suggests adnexal pathology (cyst, torsion). Sudden severe pain suggests ruptured mass.

⚠️  EMERGENCY WARNING SIGNS / RED FLAGS:
If patient reports any of the following, urgent action required:

  🚨 High fever with severe pain - tubo-ovarian abscess possible

  🚨 Sudden severe pain with peritonitis - ruptured abscess, call 911

  🚨 Pregnant with pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) symptoms - ectopic pregnancy must be ruled out

  🚨 Hypotension or sepsis - ruptured tubo-ovarian abscess, call 911
